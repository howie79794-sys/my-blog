Cybersecurity and Financial Stability

韦斯利·格雷◆ 原文出处 ↗ 2026-11-10 · 1204 words

Banks have traditionally been concerned with financial risks such as credit losses, liquidity shortages, and market volatility. But as financial institutions become increasingly dependent on digital infrastructure, a different source of vulnerability is emerging: cybersecurity. A cyberattack may initially appear to be a technology problem.

Yet when banks rely on interconnected payment systems, cloud services, and third-party technology providers, a disruption affecting one institution can spread to others. What begins as an operational failure may ultimately undermine confidence in the financial system. This raises an important question: Are individual banks investing enough in cybersecurity to protect not only themselves but also the broader financial system? And could the threat of a bank run change how much protection banks are willing to provide?

Key Academic Insights

Cybersecurity Is a Public Good and the Weakest Link Matters

Banks increasingly depend on shared digital infrastructure. While this reduces costs, it also creates vulnerabilities that extend beyond individual institutions.

An attacker who compromises one bank may exploit weaknesses in a shared service, potentially disrupting other institutions.
Cybersecurity therefore resembles a weakest-link public good. Investments by one bank can protect others, while inadequate defenses at one institution can weaken the entire network.

This creates a problem: banks may not fully consider the benefits their cybersecurity investments provide to the broader financial system.

Banks Have Incentives to Underinvest in Cybersecurity

The authors identify a classic economic problem: free-riding.

Because cybersecurity investments benefit other institutions, banks may prefer to rely partly on protection provided by their peers.
Individually, this can seem rational. Cybersecurity is expensive, and not all its benefits accrue to the bank making the investment.
Collectively, however, these decisions can leave the financial system insufficiently protected.

The model shows that decentralized cybersecurity decisions may produce inefficient outcomes. Importantly, the inefficiency does not always involve underinvestment.

The Threat of Bank Runs Can Encourage Greater Cybersecurity Investment

A surprising finding is that the possibility of a bank run can encourage banks to strengthen their cybersecurity defenses. A successful cyberattack may disrupt operations and undermine creditor confidence. Depositors or other creditors could respond by withdrawing funds or refusing to renew financing.

Banks anticipating these consequences have stronger incentives to prevent attacks. The authors therefore identify two opposing forces: free-riding discourages cybersecurity investment, while the threat of a run encourages it. When rollover risk is sufficiently high, banks may even invest more in cybersecurity than would be socially optimal.

Cybersecurity and Operational Resilience Are Not the Same Thing

The paper distinguishes between preventing cyberattacks and limiting their consequences.

Cybersecurity investments reduce the likelihood of a successful intrusion. Operational resilience measures, such as backup systems and recovery procedures, help institutions continue functioning after an attack.

Banks must allocate resources between these two objectives.

The model predicts that more intense cyber threats can encourage greater investment in operational resilience. Higher rollover risk, by contrast, strengthens incentives for preventative cybersecurity.

The implication is important: preventing attacks and preparing for disruptions are complementary objectives, but they compete for limited resources.

Financial Regulation Can Help Address Cybersecurity Externalities

Because banks share infrastructure, their cybersecurity decisions affect one another. The authors examine how regulation can help align individual incentives with system-wide financial stability. Potential measures include minimum cybersecurity standards, negligence rules, cyber hygiene requirements, and regulations influencing bank funding structures.

The paper also highlights the importance of understanding connections between banks and technology providers. Mapping these dependencies could support cybersecurity stress tests and help regulators identify vulnerabilities that individual institutions might overlook.

Practical Applications for Investment Advisors

Include Cybersecurity in Financial Institution Risk Assessments

Financial institution analysis traditionally emphasizes capital adequacy, profitability, credit quality, and liquidity.
This research suggests that cybersecurity and operational resilience deserve attention alongside these measures.
A financially sound institution may still face serious operational vulnerabilities.

Advisors evaluating financial sector investments should therefore consider cybersecurity governance, technology dependencies, and business continuity arrangements.

Look Beyond Individual Institutions to Shared Technology Providers
Portfolio diversification does not necessarily eliminate exposure to shared technological vulnerabilities. Different banks, custodians, and financial service providers may rely on the same digital infrastructure. A disruption affecting a critical provider could therefore influence several institutions simultaneously. Advisors should recognize this potential concentration risk, particularly when evaluating financial sector exposures.

Evaluate Preparedness for Disruptions, Not Just Prevention
Cybersecurity assessments often focus on preventing unauthorized access. The paper highlights the equally important role of operational resilience. Advisors conducting institutional due diligence can examine recovery procedures, backup systems, and business continuity plans. The relevant question is not only whether an institution can prevent an attack, but whether it can continue operating when prevention fails.

Recognize Cyber Risk as a Potential Financial Stability Scenario

Cyberattacks can interact with traditional financial risks. A prolonged disruption could undermine confidence, trigger withdrawals, and create liquidity pressures. For advisors involved in portfolio construction or scenario analysis, this suggests considering cyber-related operational shocks alongside conventional market and credit risks.

The objective is not to predict a cyber-induced financial crisis, but to recognize how technological disruptions could amplify existing financial vulnerabilities.

How to Explain This to Clients

“Banks can be financially strong and still face serious problems if their technology stops working. Today, financial institutions rely on interconnected digital systems. A cyberattack affecting one bank or technology provider could therefore disrupt several institutions. This research explains how such disruptions might become financial problems. If creditors lose confidence, they may withdraw funds, potentially creating liquidity pressures. The study also shows why cybersecurity requires cooperation. One bank’s security decisions can affect the safety of others. For investors, the lesson is that financial stability depends on more than strong balance sheets. It also requires secure technology and the ability to recover from disruptions.”

Abstract

Cyber risk exposes banks to operational disruptions that can trigger runs. A bank chooses its cybersecurity by trading off protection against attacks with remaining resilient if an attack succeeds. Cybersecurity functions as a risk-management decision: it reduces the bank’s exposure to adverse outcomes but entails lower balance-sheet returns. Equilibrium cybersecurity depends on whether failure is driven by insolvency or illiquidity. When failure is insolvency-driven, bank and creditor actions reinforce one another: greater cybersecurity leads to a higher debt burden, which strengthens incentives for protection. When failure is illiquidity-driven, additional cybersecurity lowers the debt burden, eliminating the bank’s private risk–return trade-off. Socially optimal cybersecurity differs from the private choice, and corrective instruments must target either the protection or resilience margins. We extend the model to a system-wide environment in which cybersecurity is a public good, highlighting free-riding and the need for targeted regulation.
