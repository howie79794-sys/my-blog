Gating Functions

Doomberg◆ 原文出处 ↗ 2026-10-31 · 522 words

“We’re celebrating the greatest oil victory in history. To the victor belong the spoils, right?” – Donald J. Trump

In the late summer of 2021, heavy oil production in Venezuela’s Orinoco Belt temporarily crashed by 25%. The proximate cause of the decline was the need to divert lighter grades of domestically produced crude to the country’s struggling refineries. Those lighter hydrocarbons would normally have been used as diluents to enable transporting Orinoco’s otherwise solid-like oil sludges to markets near and far. Absent the lights, production of the heavies had to be curtailed.

The root cause of the mini-crisis was the cumulative effect of years of suffocating sanctions levied against Venezuela by successive US administrations that made external sourcing of materials, capital goods, and investor money progressively more difficult. In the final weeks of 2025, such financial weapons were supplemented with floating ones, as the US Navy imposed a partial blockade of oil tankers into and out of the beleaguered country. Among those inbound, a few that of Russian origin found themselves in a particularly interesting showdown:

“A tanker subject to US sanctions carrying some 300,000 barrels of naphtha from Russia entered Venezuelan waters late on Thursday, while another began redirecting course in the Atlantic Ocean, ship tracking data showed, a reflection of diverging last-minute decisions by ship owners after President Donald Trump ordered a ‘blockade’ of oil tankers under sanctions bound for the OPEC country earlier this week.

The move ramped up pressure on Venezuelan president Nicolas Maduro by targeting the country’s main source of income and followed the seizure by the US of an oil tanker off the coast of Venezuela earlier in December.”

The naphtha en route from Russia was itself meant to serve as diluent, and Venezuela’s inability to source it in sufficient quantities was once again contributing to precipitously falling production. The escalating pressure campaign that followed culminated in the US Army’s elite Delta Force successfully snatching President Nicolás Maduro in early January, a jarring inflection point in the long-standing geopolitical skirmish between the two countries.

Maduro’s abrupt departure left behind a government that was more pliant to US President Donald Trump’s demands. Much of the sanctions pressure exited with the former strongman, and the flow of diluent in and heavy crude out rebounded markedly. According to a recent report delivered to acting Venezuelan President Delcy Rodríguez last week, oil production reached a remarkable 1.25 million barrels per day (bpd)—up from a low of just 830,000 bpd in December—due to higher production in the Orinoco Belt and conventional crude production gains.

If one closely examines the political and molecular mayhem that has so characterized the Venezuelan oil industry over the past decade, powerful evidence emerges for many of the core themes regularly argued in these pages. Included among them are proof of the shifting molecular definition of oil itself, a proper ranking of politics ahead of geology in the hierarchy of constraints on oil production, and—perhaps most importantly—a window into the true hydrocarbon potential of the Western Hemisphere. Let’s head to Caracas to explore each in order.
