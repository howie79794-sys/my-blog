The Internet of Gas

Doomberg◆ 原文出处 ↗ 2026-11-19 · 617 words

“Poetry is more a threshold than a path.” – Seamus Heaney

When Kinder Morgan and its venture partners originally constructed the Rockies Express (REX) pipeline between 2006 and 2009, their goal was to move 1.8 billion cubic feet per day (Bcf/d) of natural gas produced in Colorado and Wyoming. The developers terminated the pipeline near the sleepy, 280-resident Ohio River village of Clarington, where the gas could enter the Texas Eastern Transmission and Dominion Transmission pipelines on its journey to numerous final destinations.

Just as the project was reaching completion, an unanticipated surge of natural gas production in the Marcellus and Utica shales was taking shape. As the Appalachian region quickly became one of the world’s most prolific producers of the fuel, industry players realized that REX flows could be reversed, providing much-needed egress for a part of the country now drowning in supply. With the opening of the Seneca Lateral in 2014, Appalachian gas began linking up with REX in Clarington and flowing to delivery points in Ohio, Indiana, and Illinois.

Among those joining the Clarington party was EQT, which brought its Ohio Valley Connector online in 2016 with 850 million cubic feet per day (MMcf/d) of Appalachian capacity. Its 400 MMcf/d Clarington Connector is set to become operational any day now, and the company is planning to terminate its 1 Bcf/d POWER Pipeline there in 2030. During a recent earnings call, executives from another eager participant, Antero Midstream, signaled that it too might add to the fast-developing Clarington hub:

“Antero Midstream (AM) has a new pipeline on the drawing board. During Thursday’s 3Q26 earnings call, CEO Michael Kennedy said AM wants to build a North-South pipeline from Antero Resources’ West Virginia field north to the Clarington, Ohio, market. Kennedy said AM is ‘scoping it right now’ and spending ‘a little initial capital’ to assess it, and that ‘you’ll probably see us FID that in 2027.’ (FID means final investment decision, the point where a company commits to build.) There was no press release. The news came out in answer to a question from a Goldman Sachs analyst.”

Clarington’s journey from a village few had ever heard of to one of the most important nodes in America’s rapidly developing natural gas network is testimony to the sheer scale and pace of one of the most complex energy buildouts ever constructed. The country’s staggering array of trunklines, laterals, and spurs almost magically connects abundant methane brought up from deep underground to the countless machines spread across the economy that burn it to do work. A recent study by S&P Global’s Platts indicates that such midstream momentum is unlikely to abate anytime soon:

“Midstream companies have stepped up natural gas pipeline projects in 2026 to connect production to an expected surge in demand from LNG terminals, power generators and data centers.

Platts, part of S&P Global Energy, has tracked 46.5 billion cubic feet/day of proposed pipeline projects announced so far in 2026. About 20 Bcf/d of this year’s projects originate in Texas.

Announcements have ranged from long-haul Permian Basin egress pipelines to links between Texas and Louisiana’s Gillis hub and laterals to individual data center campuses.”

In the midst of such seemingly spontaneous network formations, one is well-served to step back and laterally ponder the attributes of the end state. What does the US natural gas complex look like in a decade or two? How will the forces of arbitrage dictate what must eventually emerge? Can the US parlay this huge energy advantage into sustained geopolitical leverage by pressing its lead in artificial intelligence (AI)? Let’s put a few predictive stakes in the ground.
