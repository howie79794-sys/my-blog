Attrition Pocket

Doomberg◆ 原文出处 ↗ 2026-11-07 · 510 words

“The two most powerful warriors are patience and time.” – Leo Tolstoy

In 1983, the Soviet Union began constructing the 4,100-foot-long Southern Bridge in Ukraine’s capital city of Kyiv. Also known locally as the Pivdennyi Bridge, the span opened to road traffic in 1990 and metro service in 1992—years that bookend Ukraine’s declaration of independence in the messy aftermath of the Soviet Union’s collapse. As one of a handful of large bridges crossing the Dnipro River—a majestic expanse that runs the full length of Ukraine from north to south, effectively cutting the country in two—the structure is of outsized importance to military planners on both sides of the war:

“Almost everything that comes in from Ukraine’s western partners, from shells and missiles to armoured vehicles and fuel, must eventually cross the river to reach the front in the east.

Kyiv sits at the heart of this system. The main rail and road routes from the west converge here, while routes from the left bank fan out towards Chernihiv, Sumy, Poltava and Kharkiv. The capital is therefore both a gateway for Western aid and a rear base for operations to the north and east.”

Since the earliest weeks of the war in 2022, hardliners within Russia have at times demanded to know why Moscow declined to destroy these crucial logistical links. Some speculated that the bridges were harder to destroy than many understood, while others lamented Russian President Vladimir Putin’s soft touch in his prosecution of the conflict. A month ago, it became clear that the Kremlin had decided the time was finally ripe for a change in tactics:

“Russia struck a bridge linking two sides ​of the Ukrainian capital Kyiv for the second successive day, officials said on Friday, part of an intensifying campaign of air attacks on infrastructure ‌that is upending daily life.

An evening drone strike on the closed Southern Bridge was the latest in a series of attacks since Thursday that forced the full or partial suspension of traffic across it and two other bridges spanning the vast Dnipro River.”

In the ensuing weeks, continued attacks on a number of Dnipro bridges have been reported in open sources, executed with a combination of advanced drones, powerful missiles, and even bombs dropped from Russian aircraft. Official confirmation of the impact of this escalation is understandably difficult to come by, but judging from the worried commentary emerging from unofficial Ukrainian channels, the complete severing of the major bridges would indeed present a serious challenge for the armies fighting Russia on the eastern front.

Under the premise that Putin is more cunning and sinister than dovish and tepid, and broadening the framework of analysis from the war in Ukraine to the larger global conflict that includes the war against Iran, we have developed an alternative explanation for why Russia waited so long to split Ukraine in two. If we are right, it foretells an imminent climax to what we have modeled as World War III.
