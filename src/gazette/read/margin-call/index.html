Margin Call

Doomberg◆ 原文出处 ↗ 2026-10-24 · 624 words

“We choose mania over boredom every time.” – James Gleick

In late August, the Errea Wittu arrived in Guyanese waters. The floating production, storage, and offloading (FPSO) magic oil boat will join four others already extracting hydrocarbons from the Stabroek Block, Big Oil’s glistening crown jewel du jour. Once full operations begin in December, total production in Guyana will reach more than one million barrels per day (bpd), or roughly one barrel for every resident of that formerly impoverished nation. This is up from no barrels less than a decade ago.

For all the withering criticism the government of Guyana received from the keep-it-in-the-ground climate crowd over the allegedly one-sided nature of the deal it cut with Stabroek’s operator, ExxonMobil, the country has transformed from one of the world’s poorest to one of its fastest-growing economies almost overnight. According to the International Monetary Fund, the country’s real GDP was just $5.5 billion in 2020. It grew 20% the next year, followed by an astonishing 63% the year after. In 2023, 2024, and 2025, the Guyanese economy swelled another 34%, 44%, and 19%, respectively. By the time it exited 2025, GDP had reached $27 billion, a fivefold increase in just five years.

And 2026? The IMF estimates another 22%.

As invariably happens when the champagne fizz of treasure is uncorked, neighboring regions have received some of the spillover: A race to drill in the waters off Suriname has yielded respectable results. Although not yet on par with the heady bounty of Stabroek, the GranMorgu development in Block 58 will soon be home to an FPSO of its own, with production of 220,000 bpd targeted for mid-2028.

If two points make a line, it is not all that difficult to grab a ruler and extend it a little farther east, and the success achieved in one part of the Equatorial Atlantic geological realm has sparked renewed interest in the Equatorial Margin off Brazil’s northern coast. Long known to have significant oil potential, the area has primarily been subject to drilling in the shallower waters closer to shore. A history of spotty results and fierce opposition from environmentalists to further exploration dampened industry enthusiasm for the area’s prospects.

In mid-August, Petrobras, the state-controlled integrated oil and gas company of Brazil, rekindled interest in the area after announcing preliminary results from its latest exploration work:

“The results of one of Brazil’s most anticipated wells in years show that it contains hydrocarbons in a deepwater area offshore the state of Amapá, according to Petrobras, which is the sole owner and operator of the exploration block.

The Morpho-1 well is located in Block FZA-M-59, more than 100 miles offshore and 300 miles from the mouth of the Amazon River, within Brazil’s Equatorial Margin. Petrobras hopes the frontier region will become its next major source of oil and gas production as output from the country’s prolific pre-salt basins gradually matures.

Petrobras said the well was drilled in a water depth of almost 9,500 ft, and the presence of hydrocarbons was confirmed through well logs. The company did not release further details about the quality of the discovery.”

By October, the company had discovered an additional oil-bearing layer in the same block, adding to the excitement.

As often happens in the early days of a potentially significant find, some are cautioning against prematurely drawing definitive conclusions. Given the stakes involved—and the fact that Brazil’s near-term political future will be decided in the hotly contested presidential runoff election tomorrow—there’s no better time to head south and have a look for ourselves. Will the Equatorial Margin soon become as consequential for the industry as Stabroek? Let’s hazard a guess.
