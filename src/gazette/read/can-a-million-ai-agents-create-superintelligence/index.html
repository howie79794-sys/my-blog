Can a Million AI Agents Create Superintelligence?

诺亚·史密斯◆ 原文出处 ↗ 2026-10-19 · 7128 words

Ramez Naam is, in my opinion, one of the best futurists alive. But although he has a Substack (which you should definitely follow), he doesn’t write nearly enough. So I’ve been encouraging him to write more by cross-posting his posts on my blog.

Somewhat to my surprise, Ramez has turned out to be a lot more of an AI skeptic than I would have though. He’s certainly not dismissive of the technology, but he disagrees with many of the more expansive and dramatic claims coming out of the big labs (especially Anthropic). In his first post in this series, he expressed skepticism that recursive self-improvement (RSI) would lead to a fast takeoff of AI capabilities in the near future:

In this post, he expresses skepticism about the potential for agent swarms — large numbers of AI agents working in parallel — to offer dramatic increases in AI capabilities. Agent swarms have been in the news a lot, both because of the Hugging Face attack, and OpenAI’s solution of the famous Navier-Stokes problem is also widely attributed to a large swarm. But Ramez argues that parallelization is just another kind of input that suffers from diminishing returns, and that we should therefore temper our expectations about what agents swarms are able to achieve.

As was the case with his previous post, I’m not sure how much this matters, when the capabilities of the underlying models — swarm or no swarm — are still improving at such a steady and impressive rate. But it’s a very intellectually interesting argument, and Ramez can be very persuasive.

In my previous post, we asked whether AIs could improve themselves all the way to absurdly high levels of superintelligence. My conclusion: today’s feedback loop is probably too weak, because improving AI faces diminishing returns at every step. Even fully autonomous AIs doing all the research and coding don’t make those diminishing returns disappear. We should keep updating that conclusion as real-world parameters and data change.

But we didn’t look closely at another possibility: running millions or billions of AI minds in parallel. How much do they help? That’s what we’ll look at here.

TL;DR

Plenty of people have modeled how more AI agents working in parallel could fuel recursive self-improvement. But the data we have today tell a different story. More agents are useful for speed: they can solve many problems faster. They’re less useful for boosting AI intelligence. Multi-agent gains in solving harder problems often diminish incredibly quickly and may even saturate. Whether multi-agent systems can produce the conceptual breakthroughs RSI might require to become self-sustaining is the harder question.

Without breakthroughs in how well agents parallelize problems - something that may depend on the structure of machine learning problems themselves - it’s hard to see even millions or billions of copies of today’s models making repeated breakthroughs on the hardest problems. These are among the strongest diminishing returns we’ve looked at. Of course, these diminishing returns may change. And we have less experimental data to go off of today than we’d like. We’ll need to recalibrate as new evidence comes in. I’ll end with the things we should track and my predictions about a natural experiment from newly released Anthropic data.

What we’ve already covered regarding RSI and diminishing returns

To recap the last post: recursive self-improvement (RSI) means AI improvements feeding back into the next round of AI research. Four findings stood out:

  • Real AI research is harder than benchmark scores suggest. OpenAI’s models needed human help on much shorter research tasks than the coding benchmarks led us to expect.

  • More AI activity produces much smaller gains in useful work. OpenAI used 124 times as many tokens per researcher but ran only 1.6 times as many experiments per researcher.

  • Maintaining progress requires ever more resources. Training and thinking time face steep diminishing returns. Anthropic reports that more powerful AI has been key to maintaining the current pace of AI progress.

  • The measured software feedback loop doesn’t yet look self-sustaining. Our tentative calibration put it roughly five to ten times below the strength needed.

RSI doesn’t necessarily mean runaway superintelligence

I want to reiterate a distinction here: fully automated AI self-improvement doesn’t at all guarantee an intelligence explosion. Diminishing returns in AI capabilities dampen that feedback loop. Parallel agents can help automate a search across AI parameters, but don’t look nearly as useful in making new conceptual breakthroughs. My expectation is that we’ll need those sorts of breakthroughs for self-improvement to become self-sustaining, let alone to accelerate rapidly and reach truly profound levels of superintelligence.

Even a billion parallel AIs also face diminishing returns

Today, let’s examine in much more detail what it would mean to run thousands, millions, or billions of AI researchers in parallel.

In this post I’m focusing only on software: AI researchers improving the algorithms that make AI better or cheaper to run. Hardware, investment, and economic growth have their own feedback loops, which we discussed briefly in the last post. Here, we’re asking how much more the AI minds themselves can discover.

A country of geniuses in a datacenter

In Machines of Loving Grace, Dario Amodei imagines a:

“country of geniuses in a datacenter”

Millions of AI minds, each more capable than a Nobel Prize winner across many fields, working at much higher speed. That would be extraordinary. The intelligence of each mind is a crucial part of the premise.

Tom Davidson and Daniel Eth’s Forethought paper describes the equivalent of “millions of top-notch human researchers.” Benjamin Todd’s explainer points to “millions of competent engineers working on AI research.” Dwarkesh Patel emphasizes AI’s “massive population size and perfect knowledge transfer.”

How does the number of AI agents running in parallel enter these arguments? In Eth and Davidson’s illustrative model, twice as many researchers produce twice as many papers. Cheaper AI lets the same hardware run more researchers, while each software improvement gets harder to find. Alan Chan and colleagues’ 2026 paper, including AI pioneers Geoffrey Hinton and Yoshua Bengio, assumes an even larger effect from adding researchers: doubling effective research labor increases the rate of software improvement by about 2.6 times, holding the software level fixed.

Those are assumptions about what more researchers produce. The growing number of AI researchers must keep finding improvements fast enough to outweigh the increasing difficulty of further progress. We’ll come back to those assumptions after looking at what larger AI teams actually achieve.

Meanwhile, we had OpenAI’s Navier–Stokes announcement: around 10,000 agents working concurrently during an 88-hour run solved a mathematics Millenium Problem. It made the idea vivid. A datacenter full of AI mathematicians, tackling a famously hard problem.

I understand the excitement. But the number of agents tells us how many AI researchers we ran. We also need to measure what those additional researchers contributed.

Not all diminishing returns are alike

On MTS Live, I described a ladder of increasingly hard scaling:

  • Super-linear: Twice the input produces more than twice the output. Accelerating returns.

  • Linear: Twice the input produces twice the output. Independent jobs can look like this. A power-law exponent of 1 is the same case.

  • Power-law diminishing returns: Output grows as input raised to a power below 1. At exponent 0.5, doubling output takes four times the input. At 0.2, it takes 32 times. At 0.1, it takes 1,024 times.

  • Logarithmic: Each multiplication of input buys the same additional gain. Another five points might require another tenfold increase in compute.

  • Sub-logarithmic: Even successive tenfold increases buy smaller gains. Those gains can keep shrinking, or saturate at a ceiling. A slow rise doesn’t require a hard plateau.

Not all diminishing returns are alike. Some are much harder than others.

The classic pre-training scaling laws describe power-law reductions in prediction loss. Many test-time score curves look roughly logarithmic over the measured range. Some multi-agent results flatten much more sharply.

Let’s illustrate this with numbers. In the figure below, every curve earns the same gain from the first doubling of input. The later differences then become visible. The hardest curves share a vertical scale, and each panel states its endpoint.

Different diminishing returns scaling patterns lead to very different long term results.

For runaway RSI, improvements have to make subsequent improvements arrive faster and faster. Logarithmic returns to the number of AI researchers are too weak to sustain that loop if every further doubling of software efficiency requires a fixed percentage more research. Sub-logarithmic scaling or a plateau are weaker still. The experiments I’ll summarize in the rest of this post aren’t perfect, and I wish we had more data. But the clearest larger-team evidence we have today points to logarithmic or sub-logarithmic intelligence gains. Those look too weak to make RSI self-sustaining, let alone runaway, under the assumptions we’re examining.

What limits multiagent scaling

There are at least three reasons a larger team might be limited in its ability to produce better answers, and probably more. Let’s start with these:

First, coordination skills. Can the agents divide the problem, manage the work, check each other, and combine their results? Can they communicate what matters without spending all their time communicating? A team needs good project management as well as good individual contributors.

Second, homogeneity of thought. Are the agents contributing useful new ideas, or repeating the same approaches and mistakes? A hundred copies can share the same blind spots. More varied answers help only if some of that variety is right.

Third, the structure of the problem. How much can happen in parallel, and how much has to wait for earlier results? A million independent searches can run at once. A sequential investigation can’t. Even figuring out how to divide a conceptual problem may require an insight the team hasn’t found.

Three places where more agents can run into limits.

These limits interact. A better model can supply an idea its copies previously lacked, find a better division of labor, or recognize a solution the team had been overlooking. A difficult task may become easier to parallelize once somebody discovers the right approach. That’s why the shape of the curve matters, and why it can change.

Before we go further, let’s separate two things: speed, or how quickly agents solve a problem, and collective intelligence, or whether they can solve a harder problem and reach a new level of skill. We’ll use verified benchmark scores as evidence of that intelligence. They aren’t a universal IQ scale.

So far, adding agents seems much more effective at speeding up execution than at raising the level of problem a team can solve.

So far, using more agents buys us much more speed than extra capability.

Agents can buy you speed at a higher cost

Let’s start with the best case. A million independent jobs. A million candidates to test. A million places to search. Each agent gets useful work it can do without waiting for the others, and a reliable way to check whether it succeeded.

That can scale extremely well. Ten workers may finish ten jobs in the time one worker finishes one. A huge search may find a rare solution that a smaller search misses entirely.

One concerning use of parallel agents is offensive cybersecurity. Lisan al-Ghaib emphasizes this possibility. I disagree with his broader argument about swarm intelligence, but many attacks running in parallel could be extremely powerful. A swarm may not need a conceptual breakthrough to search many targets or try many possible vulnerabilities at once.

That said, penetrating the most secure targets, such as operating systems and browsers, can require long attack chains. Each step depends on earlier discoveries to get through another layer of defenses. Here again, we might find diminishing returns or eventual saturation, even with respect to speed. Indeed, one of the experiments below suggests that.

The speed-cost tradeoff

Toby Ord analyzed how well GPT-5.6 Sol increases the speed of solving problems on different benchmarks, and at what cost. This gives us a useful way to measure the tradeoff. In general, using more agents can help get tasks done more quickly, but at higher total cost than if you’d used fewer agents.

On top of that, the tradeoff varies by the type of task. On command-line tasks (Terminal-Bench), Ord’s analysis finds that four agents can reach the same result about 1.9 times faster, at about 2.1 times the token cost. On web research (BrowseComp), four agents can reach the same result about 2.6 times faster, at about 1.6 times the cost. I’m using the figure below to show how different tasks differ in that speed vs cost tradeoff.

More speed costs more, and the tradeoff varies by task. Based on Toby Ord’s analysis. Source.

Why the difference? Web research can often split into independent searches. A command-line task may have to change a shared environment in sequence. Some problems are simply easier to divide among agents than others.

Ord uses lambda, λ, for how speed scales with the number of agents. We’ll come back to it. At λ = 0.5, speed and total compute cost rise by the same factor. A higher λ means speed rises faster than cost.

Opus 5.5 gets more speed out of parallel agents than older models

Inspired by Toby Ord’s analysis, I applied the same approach to Anthropic’s published results on program reconstruction, or ProgramBench. Here again, more speed comes with more cost. But we also see that models within the Claude family are getting better at the speed-cost tradeoff. The newest model, Opus 5.5, gets more speedup for a smaller cost increase than Opus 5 or Fable 5.1.

With five agents, Opus 5.5 gets about 2.7 times its solo speed, compared with 2.2 times for Opus 5, and does so at lower cost. [Note: We’re using total agent runtime as a proxy for cost. Token usage and dollar costs could differ.]

Opus 5.5 gets more speedup for a smaller cost increase than older AI models. Source.

Speed gains probably get harder as we add more agents

Will these speed-versus-cost gains hold up at hundreds, thousands, or millions of agents? Probably not completely, and certainly not for all tasks.

Some problems are easy to split ten ways, harder to split a hundred ways, and harder still to split among ten thousand agents. More of the work may have to wait for earlier results, and coordinating all those pieces takes work too. Smarter models can get better at this. But my best guess is that, on many problems, each further expansion will buy less speed for the added cost.

Anthropic’s formal math proof benchmark gives us one example.

On the Lean formal math task, we can compare how quickly teams reach the same score. Using Anthropic’s published checkpoints, we estimate the speed and cost of reaching 0.50. The chart starts with ten agents as the baseline.

On a math proof benchmark, the speed advantage of adding additional agents starts to taper with scale. Source.

From ten to thirty agents, speed improves faster than cost. From thirty to a hundred, cost rises faster than speed. The local speed exponent, λ, falls from about 0.75 to 0.33. The extra agents still save time, but the tradeoff gets worse.

This suggests that even speed gains can taper as we split the work among more agents, let alone gains in intelligence. More agents may add less and less as we approach the limits of how easily a problem divides into subproblems. Better coordination may push those limits outward; the task itself may still impose them.

Intelligence gains face much harder diminishing returns than speed

So in speed, more agents can buy us time at some additional cost. Newer models are improving, but there are probably limits. What about intelligence?

Most arguments for superintelligence go beyond running huge numbers of today’s agents. They posit individual models becoming vastly smarter. There’s a chicken-and-egg problem here. Getting there may require conceptual breakthroughs in machine learning itself, perhaps as profound as the ideas that made modern machine learning possible, or at least as important as the Transformer and reasoning models. Parallel search may not be enough to find them. Anthropic has identified ambitious research ideas as an area where current models lag behind humans.

In the previous post, I argued that today’s measured gains from smarter models are roughly five to ten times too weak to produce a self-sustaining feedback loop. But what about running enormous numbers of our best models in parallel? Can a massive team of frontier agents that aren’t yet superintelligent invent a new architecture, a new learning method, or the idea needed to build a much smarter successor? That’s the heart of the question.

For runaway RSI, those discoveries have to overcome the diminishing returns we discussed in the last post. Here are three places they appear.

The diminishing returns a runaway research loop has to change or overcome.

The pre-training example comes from the Chinchilla scaling fit. The reinforcement-learning example comes from ScaleRL. The inference row describes the test-time math curves we discussed previously. The goal is better returns to data and compute, and gains that continue longer before flattening.

My expectation is that we’ll get easy AI improvements through work we can automate, parallelize, and search. But to get very large benefits, and runaway RSI, I expect we’ll need conceptual breakthroughs. That’s why the intelligence of each model matters so much.

Bear in mind that we’ve observed these sorts of diminishing returns in machine learning for more than thirty years. Power-law learning curves were measured in 1993. They appear to be a fundamental feature of machine learning as we know it. We shouldn’t assume they’ll be trivially changed, or that we’ll suddenly find a way to improve AI without diminishing returns. That would be a revolution. Discovering such a route might itself require far smarter minds, and we don’t know whether the mathematics of learning would permit it across the problems we care about.

That doesn’t mean there haven’t been big improvements. The transformer and reasoning models have moved the field forward enormously. But, as we saw in the previous post, a runaway feedback loop needs enough acceleration to keep outweighing the obstacles, again and again. As ideas get harder to find, we may need a faster research pace just to maintain today’s rate of progress. Anthropic’s assessment suggests that better AI tools are becoming necessary just to maintain the current pace of AI progress.

Example one: GPT-5.6 cybersecurity gains shrink with more agents

OpenAI’s GPT-5.6 Sol results provide a useful example on cybersecurity. SEC-Bench Pro tests the generation of proof-of-concept demonstrations of software vulnerabilities. This is a particular security task, not a measure of coding ability in general. The reported peak scores are approximately 71.2 with one agent, 74.3 with four, and 76.4 with sixteen.

On this cybersecurity test, sixteen times as many agents buy about 5.2 score points. The first fourfold increase adds 3.1 points; the next adds only 2.1. There’s still plenty of room below a perfect score, but the gain on this test is shrinking, even with multiplication of the number of agents. This is sub-logarithmic scaling.

On SEC-Bench Pro, each expansion buys fewer score points, showing sub-log scaling. Source.

These agents could coordinate through OpenAI’s ultra setup. This is already a test of collaboration, not just a stack of independent guesses. The points are the best reported results across different budgets, rather than an equal-compute comparison.

Example two: A smarter model did more for OpenAI’s math results than more agents

When OpenAI announced its Navier–Stokes result, the 10,000 agents attracted enormous excitement. Interviewing Noam Brown, Dwarkesh Patel said he was “shocked by the scale of cognitive effort” concentrated into such a short run.

But how much did those extra agents contribute? Brown, an OpenAI researcher who helped develop o1 and now works on multi-agent systems, was emphatic:

“I wouldn’t even attribute 10% of the credit to multi-agent.”

He credits the smarter underlying model. OpenAI hadn’t run the single-agent experiment needed to isolate the swarm’s contribution. Brown’s estimate is a judgment, but it directly challenges the idea that the sheer number of agents was the decisive ingredient.

Example three: Formal math proof gains diminish sharply after ten Opus 5.5 agents

Formal mathematical work seems especially analogous to the conceptual breakthroughs AI research may need. It demands a chain of valid reasoning, sometimes with a new insight that makes the whole problem tractable. Formalizing existing mathematics isn’t the same as inventing a new learning algorithm, but it’s a useful place to look.

And here we find almost no extra gain when Opus 5.5 grows from ten parallel agents to a hundred. Anthropic’s system card tests teams on 49 weighted theorem statements, checked in Lean. Each team gets 24 hours. The first tenfold expansion adds 27 score points; the next adds just two.

On these math proofs, most of the gain arrives by ten agents. This is steeply sub-logarithmic scaling, and possibly saturation Source.

These agents could communicate, share a machine and Git repository, and organize their work. The hundred-agent team even developed a hierarchy with twelve sub-leads. It could also use ten times as much aggregate agent runtime as the ten-agent team over the same day. That isn’t a measured tenfold increase in tokens, but it gives the larger team much more room to work.

We still didn’t get over the hump. This won’t necessarily stay true, but the diminishing returns are incredibly steep here, possibly pointing toward a plateau.

What the formal math proof slowdown suggests

Opus 5.5 starts smarter than the older models and gets more speedup from parallel agents on ProgramBench. It clearly can put additional agents to useful work. So why do intelligence gains nearly stop on these math proofs? Two possibilities stand out:

  • The remaining work may be hard to parallelize. Later steps depend on understanding earlier results.

  • The remaining problems may need insights the model can’t find, however many copies we run. Intelligence may matter more than agent population or speed.

We haven’t isolated the cause. But a thousand copies don’t automatically supply the insight one copy is missing.

Example four: A smarter model beats ten times the agents on math proofs

Looking at the same math results, we see something striking. Ten Opus 5.5 agents get a higher score than a hundred Fable 5.1 or Opus 5.0 agents. Opus 5.5 starts much higher than the older models. It improves substantially with ten agents, then barely improves with a hundred. But at 10 it’s already better than Fable or Opus 5’s results with a hundred agents.

Opus 5.5 starts stronger, but its team gains flatten quickly. Source.

What about diversity?

Does adding different minds help? A study of model and persona diversity found substantial gains as diverse teams grew to eight agents. After that, the average across its tasks barely moved.

Diversity of AI models helps, but the gains flatten after eight to twelve agents. Source.

Other approaches, including X-MAS and Aitchison and colleagues’ forecasting study, find useful gains from combining different models. But none of these results demonstrates sustained improvement over many expansions of the team. The first study directly shows flattening; the others test smaller ranges.

It’s plausible that today’s models simply aren’t diverse enough. LLMs often produce strikingly similar ideas. Perhaps we need bigger changes in how these minds are trained. We’ll return to whether more deliberately different agents could change the curves.

The Best Examples of Multi Agents Improving Collective Intelligence

All of the above examples show sub-logarithmic scaling, roughly the hardest kind. That said, some experiments show slightly easier diminishing returns, indicating roughly the same gain from each multiplication of parallel work. That’s logarithmic scaling. It’s better, but still steep diminishing returns. If that’s how useful research grows while each further AI improvement gets harder to find, even billions of agents don’t make a self-sustaining RSI loop look plausible.

In Ryan Greenblatt’s ARC experiment, a model generated and checked many candidate programs. More samples kept improving its score, by roughly the same amount per doubling. This is parallel search rather than a communicating research team, but it gives us a useful comparison.

Parallel search keeps improving, with logarithmic returns. Source.

A molecular-exploration study found a similar logarithmic pattern as it grew from one to 128 agents. Multiple agents explored chemical targets more effectively than one agent given the same total evaluation budget. Each agent learned as it searched.

More AI agents explore more chemical targets, with logarithmic diminishing returns. Source.

There are encouraging results from communicating teams too, including Argus on web research and Microsoft and Berkeley’s work on ARC-AGI-3. The latter finds unusually strong gains from one to five agents. Those results deserve attention; we still need to see what happens with much larger teams.

Logarithmic gains are better than the sub-logarithmic curves we saw earlier. Each tenfold expansion keeps buying the same extra increment, instead of less each time. But both shapes rise drastically more slowly than the research gains assumed in hypothetical models of self-sustaining or runaway RSI. Let’s put them side by side.

What does this mean for runaway RSI

Will a million or a billion parallel AIs get us to self-sustaining or runaway RSI? Let’s do the math.

Hypothetical software-RSI models assume that a growing number of AI researchers produces a much larger stream of discoveries. Those discoveries make AI cheaper or more productive, allowing the next round of research to grow again.

There’s a strong case for gains in speed and for searching more of a well-defined space. Even speed gains, as we’ve seen, can taper. Intelligence is much harder to boost through parallel agents. In several experiments, peak problem-solving performance (what I’m referring to here as intelligence) rises dramatically more slowly than speed.

Here’s what three software-RSI models assume:

  • Chan and colleagues (2026): doubling effective research labor produces about 2.6 times the rate of software improvement, holding the software level fixed. I find that implausible as a general assumption about adding copies of today’s AI models. The clearest large-team intelligence experiments look nothing like that.

  • Eth and Davidson (2025): their illustrative team produces twice as many papers from twice as many researchers. The accelerating example assumes that the number of researchers grows faster than the work required for each further improvement.

  • Tom Davidson and Tom Houlden (2025): their central exponent for parallel research effort is 0.6. Ten times the labor gives about four times the research pace. They cut that exponent in half, to 0.3, when experimental compute is held fixed and becomes a bottleneck. The benchmark reports above don’t appear to have been limited by experimental compute, so we’re using 0.6 here.

Removing humans from the loop doesn’t remove shared blind spots or sequential dependencies. Adding more copies of today’s models hasn’t shown the sustained intelligence gains that may be needed for repeated conceptual breakthroughs.

Will agents get better at scaling?

I do expect better AI models to get better at spreading work across agents: dividing the problem, managing collaborators, and combining what they find. We’ve already seen progress in speed on program reconstruction. We haven’t seen a clear trend toward better multi-agent intelligence scaling in the math results. More on that in a moment.

First, an encouraging analogy: single-agent thinking-time scaling. Toby Ord’s analysis suggests that humans get more relative benefit from additional time than these AIs get from additional reasoning tokens. That may surprise people. But newer models are getting closer to the human reference on these cybersecurity tasks.

AI agents can get stuck in cognitive ruts, repeating or refining an approach that isn’t getting them far. Newer models seem to be improving at how they use extended thinking.

Newer models make better use of extra thinking tokens on these cyber tasks. Source.

This isn’t multi-agent scaling, but it’s encouraging. AIs were poor at turning more thinking into harder solved tasks, and that scaling parameter improved. They might eventually pass the human reference. That’s part of why I don’t see runaway RSI as imminent, yet think it could become more plausible as these capabilities change.

We’ve also seen new findings on helping agents escape their ruts and use more thinking time productively. The new Fork-and-Flush study periodically creates parallel branches that keep the accumulated work but start with fresh chat contexts, then continues from the best branch. It improved results across thirteen research and engineering tasks at the same compute budget. That’s a concrete research direction for using additional work more effectively.

So, have we seen a similar improvement in multi-agent scaling? For speed, sometimes. For intelligence, there’s no clear direction, so far, at least on formal math proofs. Opus 5.5 gets more speedup for a smaller cost increase on program reconstruction. But the score gains in the chart below are noisy, and newer models don’t consistently get a larger boost from additional agents.

Newer models don’t get consistently large gains from larger math-proof teams. Source.

That doesn’t mean better multi-agent scaling won’t happen. I expect the labs to work hard on coordination. Improving diversity of thought is a more open research question, but I’m sure it’s at least somewhat tractable given sufficient effort. The structure of the problem may impose the hardest limits: some discoveries have to happen before the next useful step becomes possible. We don’t yet know how much each of these constraints contributes.

Summarizing what we’ve seen, here’s an update on the conceptual limits to multi-agent scaling.

What we’ve learned so far, and where the hardest limits might be.

Can we build minds that think more differently?

Frontier models share many of the same broad training ingredients: overlapping public data, related algorithms, and similar reasoning and coding exercises. So how different are these minds really?

The Artificial Hivemind study found striking similarities in models’ open-ended responses. A creativity study involving 467 people likewise found that adding AI responses eventually contributed less diversity than adding human responses. These experiments suggest that AI agents can bring less diversity of thought than an equal number of humans on these tasks, which may limit how much we get from parallel agents.

Can we make agents more different from each other? Very likely. Here are four ideas. I’m sure there are others:

  • Different training experiences: vary data mixtures, curricula, reinforcement-learning environments, and reward objectives to develop strong agents that favor different useful approaches.

  • Complementary specializations: combine agents trained for conjecturing, finding lemmas, searching for counterexamples, and checking proofs. Test whether their strengths combine into results no member reliably achieves alone.

  • Independent proposals before discussion: let agents develop approaches before seeing one another’s answers, then exchange evidence and criticism. Test whether this reduces early convergence on the same mistake.

  • Teams chosen for complementary errors: select members whose successes and failures overlap less, rather than relying only on individual benchmark rank. Measure whether the team finds more verified solutions as it grows.

How to measure agentic scaling improvements

As we discussed in the previous post, we’re going off of scant data here. This model could be wrong. Or newer AI versions could get sufficiently better at doing parallel work that these diminishing returns would change. If that happens, we need to be aware of it.

How would we track this? I’d propose measuring three things from new AI models across a variety of problem times. Across each task type, and especially across real world AI research, measure three parameters and track how they change as newer models arrive:

  • G - Intelligence scaling: How much verified task performance does each multiplication of agents add? Measure local score gains, slopes, or exponents as appropriate. This is a task-specific proxy for collective intelligence, not a universal intelligence unit.

  • λ - Speed-cost scaling: How much faster does the team reach the same result, and how much more total compute or agent-time does it use? Track the speed exponent λ over successive team-size ranges.

  • N - Where gains weaken or saturate: At what team size do the score or speed curves bend, or approach a ceiling? Does the bend or onset of saturation move outward with newer models?

Track intelligence gains, the speed-cost tradeoff, and where gains weaken or saturate.

G and N answer two different questions. How much extra benchmark score does a defined team expansion buy, and how many agents can we add before those gains flatten? Watch these parameters over time, especially G and N. Don’t stop at one to ten agents. Measure the gains from 1→10, 10→100, and 100→1,000 agents, separately, on each benchmark. A newer model might scale better over the first interval and worse over the next. We need to know whether the gains improve and whether the bend moves outward.

We especially need to track these trends for actual internal AI research. Are newer models getting bigger gains from larger teams? Are those teams producing more verified discoveries and better successor models? That’s what would tell us whether massive parallel teams are getting closer to the conceptual breakthroughs RSI may need.

The Anthropic test and what would change my mind

Anthropic has given us a concrete test to watch. Its October employment report shows research agents logging about nine hours for every human work-hour by mid-July, and nineteen by late August. The company-wide ratio reached about six.

Research agent runtime rises to about nine hours per human work-hour by mid-July and nineteen by late August. These hours include longer operation as well as parallel agents. Source.

Let’s make a generous assumption: each agent-hour is as valuable as a human research-hour, with the number of human researchers unchanged. Agents can run throughout the 168-hour week. Anthropic’s runtime ratios already count those extra operating hours against a forty-hour human week.

On that assumption, July’s nine agent-hours plus one human hour amount to ten times the labor of humans alone. August’s total is twenty times. But Anthropic already had roughly one agent-hour per human hour in April. Compared with that observed baseline, total labor grew fivefold by July and tenfold by August.

What should that do to research? Treat the added labor as parallel research effort. Davidson and Houlden’s relationships imply about 2–4 times April’s research pace by August, depending on whether experimental compute becomes a bottleneck. Applying the labor exponent used by Alan Chan and colleagues, including Geoffrey Hinton and Yoshua Bengio, gives about 25 times. Log scaling gives about twice the pace. Our sub-log shapes give only about 1.1–1.8 times.

Now translate research pace into the Epoch Capabilities Index (ECI). Suppose software research supplies half of baseline capability improvement, while the compute-growth contribution stays unchanged. Then four times the software pace means about 2.5 times the overall ECI slope, not four times. We’re already discounting the software boost. Other obstacles could reduce it further.

Why do log scaling and Davidson-Houlden’s power 0.3 nearly tie? This is only a tenfold labor increase from April to August. At that scale, both calibrations give about twice the software pace. By 100 agents and then a billion, the hypothetical power law curve pulls far ahead of the experimentally-observed log and sub-log scaling rules.

Around late May, agents were already logging about three hours per human hour: twice April’s total labor on our assumption. With a six-month lag, that earlier signal would arrive around November.

These tests have many different assumptions, including equal value per hour and treating added runtime as parallel labor. I’m open to refining them on how others think about this. In measurement, we should judge the AI pace of improvement as the rate of at which ECI score goes up over successive releases, allowing for development and publication delays.

My expectation - and how I’ll update if I’m wrong

Personally, I don’t expect to see sustained acceleration of Anthropic’s AI progress (as measured by ECI) through the first half of 2027, and certainly not one as large as these simple extrapolations from the hypothetical models of Davidson and Houlden or Chan and colleagues suggest. If anything, I expect acceleration at the low end of the sub-log scaling bar at the bottom of the chart.

I could be wrong for many reasons. Parallel search of different AI training parameters, or use of AI to code new RL environments, or gains from AIs combining existing ideas in the literature could all make a bigger difference than I expect. Or, at the next increment of AI improvement, AIs could start making much more major advances in machine learning theory that allow us to bend the scaling laws and other diminishing returns that AI faces, or to add new axis of scaling, as reasoning models did.

On the other hand, while more parallel search and recombination of existing ideas seems highly likely, they may not bring huge advantages, and the AIs we’re making today might not find conceptual breakthroughs. Atop that, ideas probably really are getting harder to find in AI, and that may chew up some or all of the increased capacity of AI to work on improving itself.

We shall see. If we see the rate of AI improvement increase significantly, I’ll update my views in proportion to what the data show.

In summary, for now, multi-agent scaling shows incredibly steep diminishing returns to model intelligence, or the ability to reach conceptual breakthroughs. I expect those sorts of breakthroughs to be necessary for major AI advances and for self-sustaining RSI, let alone a runaway intelligence explosion. And at the same time, we’re all operating on far too little real world data. The most important thing we can do now and for the months and years ahead is to rigorously collect that data, and use it to update our forecasts of the future.
