The Cost of Human Frailty: Nvidia's Jensen Huang Test!

阿斯瓦斯·达莫达兰◆ 原文出处 ↗ 2026-11-20 · 4364 words
In a month full of big stories, perhaps the biggest business story was the fall from grace of Jensen Huang and the consequences for Nvidia, investors, and the rest of the AI trade. The initial story, with its potent mix of personal fallibility and security concerns, related to Jensen’s affair with Alice Huang, who news stories labeled a Chinese spy, though the evidence on the ground is still thin. I had planned to write about the story then, but on the road in Asia and with teaching commitments, I put it off for a few days, and over those days, the story took a turn, with stories of dalliances with Nvidia employees. Last week culminated with Nvidia’s board asking Jensen to step down, and his agreeing, perhaps bringing to an end one of the more eventful and productive stints as a CEO in corporate history. I have no desire or standing to debate the moral aspects of this story, and I will let others take sides on whether the board should or should not have fired Jensen. In this post, I will focus on the question of what Jensen stepping down as CEO might mean for Nvidia’s value, and whether after the market correction over the last three weeks, the market has overreached.
What does a CEO do?What does a CEO do for a business? Given the many tasks that go into creating, building, and running a business, it should come as no surprise that the list is long, but I will classify them broadly into groups:
  1. Storyteller: Every business is built around a story, and that story drives its valuation and sometimes its pricing. While that story is grounded in the company’s products and the markets that it seeks, it is the top manager’s job to craft and tell the story to investors, employees and to consumers. Clearly, the significance of this component of a CEO’s story will shift, depending on where the company is in its life cycle, with its importance being greatest at either end of the life cycle.
  2. Business manager: It is of course CEO’s job to manage the business that they are put in charge off, but that can span the spectrum from building a business model, for young companies, to working on ways to scale up a business for high growth firms to defending a business model for mature businesses to scaling down the model, as the company enters the declining phase.
  3. People leader: Organizations are composed of people, including suppliers, employees and customers, and it is the top management’s job to lead these people, though the nature of that leadership will also shift as a company ages. Early on, when a company is still seeking out a business model and faces failure risk, it is to inspire people with vision and direction. As companies grow and mature, it is to create a corporate culture that retains employees, as competitors try to lure them away. In decline, when a company is shedding employees and shrinking, it is finding a way to do so as humanely as possible and find a soft landing, without destroying morale.
  4. Public face: For better or worse, the CEO represents the public face of the company with investors, regulators and others, and the role they play will vary across groups and across the life cycle. With investors, CEOs frame the vision/narrative for young companies and are key to drawing in fresh capital, whereas with mature firms, especially if they are publicly traded, managers interactions are with institutional investors, where they try to manage expectations and frame results. With regulators and politicians, the CEO’s role is sometimes to play defense, when their companies are in the crosshairs and facing regulatory questioning, and sometimes to play offense against competitors.
  5. Succession planner: There is a final aspect of a CEO’s job that is often ignored but can be key to extending a company’s life cycle, and that is preparing for a succession, where a new management is readied, for a handover, when it comes due. Clearly, the importance of creating succession plans increases as top managers age, and especially so at companies that are transitioning from one stage of the life cycle to the next.
In sum, while the list of roles that CEOs play and the functions that they provide is long, the role and functions that get emphasized will shift as companies move through the life cycle. Early in the life cycle, it is the visionary component that is front and center, moving on to business building and people leader as the company's business model takes form, and as the business scales and matures, the primary mission becomes playing defense (against competition, regulators and governments), and managing succession.
How much does a CEO matter?A hundred years of formalized business education has been built on the premise that CEOs matter, and that having good management at the top of a business can make the difference between success and failure. While I do not disagree with that broad premise, the effect of good management can be greater at some firms and smaller at others, with five determinants:
  1. Macro versus micro: Businesses that are driven more by macroeconomic movements, such as those in interest rates or commodity prices, will be less affected by who manages them than companies whose success can be traced to company-specific actions on which products to produce, how to price them and where to market them. Thus, management will matter more at a consumer product company than at a commodity company since the former’s success will depend upon its management decisions a great deal more with the latter.
  2. Competitive advantage: The nature of the competitive advantages possessed by a business can also affect how much management matters. In companies with long-standing and legacy competitive advantages, management has more of a caretaking role, than at companies that constantly need to reinvent their competitive advantages. While we do not intend to downplay the challenges that managers at Coca Cola and Aramco face, when managing the company, they face less of a challenge than managers at Costco, a company in an intensely competitive business facing disruption.
  3. Transition: Management matters more when businesses are at transition points, i.e., start-ups that are seeking out venture capital for the first time, or companies on the verge of an initial public offering or mature/declining firms ahead of a restructuring, since management actions can not only make a significant difference between success and failure, but investor reactions can also be governed by how much they trust management.
  4. Upside and Downside: There is one parameter that management at companies can be judged upon, and that is whether they operate in businesses where success is keyed by whether you create upside or by protecting against downside, with the former falling under the category of taking advantage of opportunities and the latter under risk management. In young companies, it is the former that will dominate, but as companies mature, the latter will become more important.
  5. Corporate Life Cycle: At the risk of generalizing, management matters more at start-ups and very young companies, since it must deliver not just on vision but on business-building than at mature firms, that have strong competitive advantages and are in good financial position, where management can be on autopilot. As firms go into decline, management will matter again, since management in denial or desperation can cost shareholders a great deal.


In sum, the CEO (and top management) always matters, but the extent to which it matters will vary across companies, with the life cycle playing a significant role.
The Value of a CEOGiven my obsession with value, it should come as no surprise that my discussion of CEOs begins by looking at the many ways that they can affect value. As I identify the multiple key person value drives, note that not all key people affect all value drivers, and the value effects can also vary not only widely across key people, but for the same key person, across time. At the risk of being labeled as a one-trick pony, I will use my intrinsic value framework, and by extension, the It Proposition, where if it does not affect cash flow or risk, it cannot affect value, to lay out the different effects a CEO can have on value:
For personnel at the top, and I include founders and CEOs, the effect on value comes from setting the business narrative, i.e., the story that animates the numbers (revenue growth, profit margins, capital intensity and risk) that drives value., and that effect, as I have noted in my earlier discussions of narrative and numbers, can be all encompassing. The effects of people lower down in the organization tend to be more focused on one or two inputs, rather than across the board, but that does not preclude the effect from being substantial. A salesperson who accounts for half the sales of a business and most of its new customers will influence value, through revenues and revenue growth, whereas an operations manager who is a supply chain wizard can have a large impact on profit margins. As someone who teaches corporate finance, I have always tried to pass on the message, especially to those who are headed to finance jobs at companies or investment banks, that of all of the players in an organization, finance people are among the most replaceable, and thus least likely to be key people, except perhaps at firms where distress is a real and imminent danger and finding financial pathways to survival (through restructuring and reorganizing) becomes the defining value trait. 
With this framework, valuing a key person or persons becomes a simple exercise, albeit one that may require complex assumption. To estimate key person value, you value the company twice, once with the key persons included, with all that they bring to it’s cash flows and value, and then again, without those key persons, reflecting the changes that will occur to value inputs:
Value of key person(s) = Value of business with key person - Value of business without key person
The CEO valuation approach, while general, can not only yield different values for different CEOs, but also generate a value effect that is negative for a CEO whose influence has become malignant.


The framework can also help explain how the value of a CEO can evolve over time, from a significant positive at one stage of an organization to neutral later or even a large negative, explaining why some key people get pushed out of organizations, including those that they may have founded.
Valuing Jensen at NvidiaUntil a few weeks ago, investors were valuing Nvidia on the assumption that Jensen Huang would continue to run the company for an extended period. As the founder of the company and the architect of its rise to a mega cap company, he was admired for his business-building prowess, but over the last five years of the AI boom, he has been far more than the CEO of a company providing the chips for the AI build-up. Investing in some of the companies in the AI chain and providing financing (directly or as guarantees) for others, he has nurtured the AI cap ex boom from which Nvidia benefits. In this section, I will start with an update on the Nvidia CEO story, before moving on to an assessment of where Nvidia is in the life cycle, I will value Nvidia, with Jensen heading it and no sandal on the horizon, as a base value, before exploring how the scandal and his stepping down as CEO could affect that value.
Updating the Nvidia CEO Storyline (so far)    The first story about Jensen's affairs broke on October 29, with the story of his relationship with Alice Huang, with mentions about a China connection. In the following week, the story stayed in the headlines, with news of more affairs with employees who worked at Nvidia and reported to him, a violation of corporate rules. With the White House chiming in,  the board had no choice but to act, and after an emergency meeting, Jensen announced on November 12 that he was stepping down as CEO. During this period, the market reacted to the news, with Nvidia losing a trillion and a half dollars in market cap:
The board's appointment of Collette Kress, Nvidia's CFO, as interim CEO has done nothing to quell the scandal, as the debate has turned to who the company would hire as a permanent CEO, with the twist that Jensen Huang could return in that role.    Accepting that Jensen Huang has been integral to Nvidia’s success so far does not mean that you can conclude that his exit as a CEO will decrease Nvidia’s value significantly, because that will depend on things that still are undecided. 
  1. The first is that while there is an interim CEO in place, a replacement has not been announced yet, and to estimate what Nvidia will lose in value from Jensen’s exit requires that we know the identity of the person who will replace him at the top of the company (and an assessment of how well he will fill that role)>
  2. The second is that it is worth remembering that even after he steps down as CEO, Jensen will own a sizable stake in the company, with allies in the company, and it is unclear how much he will work with the replacement CEO; the more willing he is to provide guidance and direction, the smaller the value effect should be. 
  3. The third is that this will be a test of the strength of the management team that Jensen has built at the company, and whether it can run the company without him directly calling the shots. 
  4. Finally, this is no longer just an intra-company issue, since the Chinese spy angle has opened the doors to congressional hearings and legal consequences, both of which can have effects on Nvidia’s operations, at least in the near term.
Hanging over all of these uncertainty is the question of whether some of Nvidia's biggest investors and aggrieved employees will engineer a return for Jensen, and what that will do for the company.
Where is Nvidia in the life cycle?In terms of chronological age, Nvidia is not a young company, at least by tech standards. It was founded in 1993 by Jensen and went public in 1999 and while it has enjoyed a niche status, it was AI that pushed the company to the top of the ladder, as its operating metrics (revenues, earnings) and market cap soared in the investing boom that has followed. There have been periods in Nvidia’s history where it looked like it was converging on being a mature growth company, but it has found ways to rediscover high growth markets in gaming and crypto, in the last decade, and AI in this one. In fact, the last five years, as Nvidia has grown its revenues at 69.42% a year from 2021 to 2026, and seen its gross and operating margins explode, it has become a unicorn – a company that has managed to look like a young growth company, but doing so at scale and profitability.
As founder of Nvidia, and CEO for its entire existence, Nvidia’s story is intertwined with Jensen Huang’s and it is undeniable that the company is constructed in his image – opportunistic in seeking out new markets and nurturing relationships with other players in the ecosystem for their benefit. I know that the board’s decision to ask Jensen to step down as CEO has evoked strong reactions on both sides. Those in support of the board’s action note that Jensen’s affairs with employees who reported to him was a flagrant violation of corporate policy, coupled with the security lapses in his other affair, merited the response. Those who oppose the action argue that the it is unclear whether Ms, Huang was a Chinese spy and that Jensen is far too critical not just to Nvidia as a company but also to the AI disruption that the company should keep him on. Both sides, though, should agree that Jensen has been an extraordinary chief executive and that Nvidia would not be the company it is today, without him at the helm.

My estimate of the value of equity for Nvidia, prior to the events of the last few weeks, is $4.8 tiillion, roughly $200 per share, a significant jump over my estimates of its equity value from a year prior, but a reflection of the company’s capacity to keep growing at scale and preserve profit margins. On October 29, the day that the story of Jensen's affair broke, the conpany's market capitalization was $5.2 trillion, about 8% above my estimated value.    Now that Nvidia's market capitalization has been marked down to $3.5 trillion, you may be tempted to conclude that the stock is significantly undervalued now, but that assumes that Nvidia's value has not changed as it has lost its CEO, and struggles with the aftermath of the scandal. To assess the effect of Jensen’s affairs and his departure from Nvidia, we look at two dimensions. The first is on the short term impact, with revenue growth and operating margins affected over the next year, and the resulting effect on value. The second is the long term impact, where revenue growth and margins in the long-term change, with a much greater effect on value:
  • In the short term, the Jensen affair can have three consequences. One, and I think that this is unlikely, is that Nvidia sees a drop off in orders from customers, affecting near term revenues. The second, perhaps more likely, is that a transition to a new CEO might delay or hold up decisions that affect revenue generation or create costs in the near term, thus putting pressure on profit margins. The third is that the legal and legislative blowback, especially with the China twist to the story, can have regulatory or legislative consequences that affect next year’s operations. No matter what the reason, if the effects of Jensen's departurre are truly temporary, the percentage change in Nvidia’s equity value is contained, with a 5- 10% drop in most scenarios.
  • In the long term, there is the possibility that without Jensen at the helm, Nvidia will operate differently, perhaps pulling back on financing of customers and easing the relentless marketing that has made them the unstoppable force in the AI business. While the wide range of value effects may seem like a copout, where you will land up on this table will depend on where you fall in terms of the uncertainties that I listed at the start of the section:
  1. Will Jensen Huang Return? While it seems unlikely that the board of directors at Nvidia will reverse their decision to fire Jensen, the door is not completely closed. Spurred on by Jensen's stated willingness to come back and pushback from large shareholders in the company who feel that the company needs him, there is a chance that he will return. (Kalshi estimates a 10% chance). 
  2. If replaced, how good will his replacement be? If Jensen is out as CEO, the final choice on who will be Nvidia’s next CEO is up in the air with internal names (including Colette Kress, the interim CEO and Ian Buck, VP for hyper scale), CEOs of other chip companies (Renee Haas, CEO of ARM, Lisa Su, CEO of AMD as well big name ex-CEOs of other large companies (Tim Cook at Apple) all in the mix. The board will be balancing the need for someone who understands the AI chip business with that of a crisis leader, who can manage the company through trying times. Rather than focus on resumes or reputations, the board should be looking for a good fit for Nvidia, as it stands today. Tim Cook, in my view, is not a good fit for Nvidia today, unless this mushrooms into a crisis and he takes the role as a temporary CEO. While knowledge of the chip business and Nvidia's internal workings is a good ingredient to have in the replacement, the real skill will be finding someone who can preserve the delicate balance of preserving an astounding profit model, built on marketing Nvidia as the indispensable piece for compute power, and a growth model, that may require financially supporting potential customers for its chips.
  3. If replaced, how much will Jensen be willing to work with his replacement? Closely linked to the choice of replacement CEO will be the role that Jensen plays in Nvidia in the months or perhaps even years to come. If the replacement CEO is someone that has worked with Jensen before, and feels comfortable listening to advice (and pushback) from him, it is likely that the value effect will be much more muted.
  4. How strong are the layers of management at Nvidia under the CEO? In the thirty four years that Jensen has run Nvidia, he has built a team, with a relatively flat organizational structure. From all accounts, the people that he has chosen to run Nvidia seem smart and competent, and many are intensely loyal to the company. The test will be whether they can find ways to deliver the same results without the spark of Jensen’s intuition and long term vision.
  5. Does the China story have legs? For me, the wild card in this story is the China twist. If Alice Huang does have official ties to the Chinese government and Nvidia has been using business fronts in Asia to sell its chips to the Chinese government, in violation of US law, the consequences will not only be more damaging but also persist no matter who is CEO and perhaps become worse if Jensen returns to his post.
If you believe that the board will find a competent replacement CEO for Nvidia, that Jensen will stay involved, even informally, in big decisions, that the strong management team will stay intact and that the China twist is overblown, the value drop will be small. If, as the weeks go through, the process becomes ugly and contentious, there is a real risk that you could get a CEO mismatch, burn bridges with the company’s founder and lose management personnel, resulting in a much greater loss of value, with any China-related scandals adding to the loss. For me, that value is about 30% below my estimated value of $4.8 trillion, yielding a breakeven value per share of around $140 per share, and I have a limit buy waiting at that price. If it happens, I will have Nvidia back in my portfolio, and if it does not, there are other stocks to buy!

A Governance Postscript
Jensen Huang has been a great CEO, but last week was a reminder that he is human, and that the same can be said about Elon Musk, Dario Amodei, Sam Altman and the other luminaries who are ensconced at the top of the biggest companies in the AI space. Thus, it is a given that they too will make mistakes, some of which they will be reluctant to confront, and even more unwilling to reverse. The essence of corporate governance is that even the very best managers need restraints to prevent overreach, and that giving them complete power in perpetuity is a recipe for regret. There is also a truth about long tenure as CEO that few are willing to acknowledge. A CEO that is newly hired may be humble and willing to listen, but the longer that CEO stays entrenched, the more likely it is that he or she will accumulate power, create boards that are rubber stamps and acquire an ego, completing the transition to Imperial CEO.
Finally, Nvidia’s travails should make us more appreciative of founders who build companies that can outlast them and find ways to make themselves dispensable. You may not like Bill Gates, but he left Microsoft, while still in his forties, and the company barely missed a beat and has gone on to scale new heights. The pictures of Jeff Bezos, at his wedding or in his speedos on a yacht may make you cringe, but to the extent that it does not make you stop buying on Amazon Prime or sell your Amazon stock, he has succeeded in building an entity that can outlive him.
