Ties That Bind

Doomberg◆ 原文出处 ↗ 2026-10-07 · 598 words

“No nation was ever ruined by trade.” – Benjamin Franklin

With a land area equal to that of Arizona and a population of just 27,000, Labrador—the onshore portion of the Canadian province of Newfoundland and Labrador (NL)—is one of the most resource-rich yet largely undeveloped regions in the Western Hemisphere. Abundant timber, iron ore, nickel, copper, cobalt, gold, rare earths, and other critical mineral prospects dot its rural landscape, and its large rivers provide enormous hydroelectric potential. Think of it as an even more thinly populated version of Alaska.

The border separating Labrador from Québec was not officially decided until March 1, 1927, when the Judicial Committee of the British Privy Council ruled largely in favour of what was then the Dominion of Newfoundland in its boundary dispute with Canada (NL would not join Canada as a province until 1949). The border, which Québec governments still do not formally recognize, largely follows the height of the land separating rivers that drain east to the Atlantic from those draining west and north toward Hudson Bay and the Arctic.

By far the largest hydroelectric power facility in Labrador is the Churchill Falls Generating Station on the Churchill River. Its 11 turbines combine for a nameplate capacity of 5.4 gigawatts (GW), vastly more electricity than local residents currently consume. Under a contract signed in 1969 and not set to expire until 2041, the substantial majority of Churchill Falls’ output is sold to Hydro-Québec for the paltry sum of 0.2 Canadian cents per kilowatt-hour (kWh). Among the beneficiaries of this arrangement are Hydro-Québec’s many customers in the US Northeast, whose solar and wind installations rely upon the dispatchability afforded by the impoundment dams to their north.

Understandably, the one-sided nature of this contract does not sit well with NL residents, and there have been several attempts to rebalance it over the years. The closest the two sides came to a new deal was a memorandum of understanding (MOU) signed in December of 2024, but politics impeded its implementation as a binding agreement, and it expired in April of 2026. Enter newly elected Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney, who hopes to resolve the tensions between the two provinces by vastly expanding the pie to be split, providing cover for renegotiating this notoriously bad deal:

“The Carney government is pumping C$10 billion in federal backing into a massive hydroelectric project in Newfoundland and Labrador, pitching the project as part of its effort to strengthen Canada’s economy against President Donald Trump’s trade threats.

The federal support will help to advance a broader C$70 billion project to upgrade the Churchill Falls generating station and develop Gull Island. Prime Minister Mark Carney said it will be the largest clean energy project in North America, one that could eventually generate enough electricity to light up the equivalent of Canada’s three largest cities.”

The Churchill Falls announcement is just one in a flurry of generationally significant energy and mineral development projects that Carney has thrown the full weight of his Liberal government behind—much to the shock of those on Canada’s political left and right alike. His transformation has been so abrupt, and has unfolded at such speed, that critics remain convinced that none of it will come to pass. Assessing the situation more broadly, however, reveals a deeper agenda that, if real, could finally unlock the sleeping giant of Canada’s gargantuan natural endowment, extracting wealth on a scale few can fathom. Let’s head north and explore the source of our optimism.
