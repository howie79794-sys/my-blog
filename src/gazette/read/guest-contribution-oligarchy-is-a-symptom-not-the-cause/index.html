Guest Contribution: “Oligarchy is a Symptom, Not the Cause”

门齐·钦◆ 原文出处 ↗ 2026-09-28 · 1338 words

Today, we present a guest post written by Jeffrey Frankel, Harpel Professor at Harvard’s Kennedy School of Government, and formerly a member of the White House Council of Economic Advisers. A shorter version was published in Project Syndicate. 

September 28, 2026 — The American political system is broken.  We should ask how it got that way and should hope that the answer could contain a clue as to how it might get back on track.

Of course, many factors are at play.   The long list includes things that cry out for reform, like gerrymandering of Congressional districts.  But most will not bear the weight of being identified as a single fundamental exogenous cause of all the others, nor explain why this has happened now, in the early decades of the 21st century.

A common theory is that the root cause of the problems plaguing US politics is heightened income inequality, which has allowed the emergence of a self-perpetuating oligarchy that is able to buy the policies it wants through political contributions.   The prescription that would follow starts by rallying to a campaign to reduce the economic gap between the richest citizens and those with lower incomes.  This probably captures the spirit of the new candidates associated with Democratic Socialists on the left.

Yes, the increase in inequality is highly undesirable.  Relatively attainable policy responses would include expanding the Earned Income Tax Credit and expanding Obamacare.   Yes, the upper .01 percent have been able to perpetuate their wealth as it is passed down to their descendants by successfully pushing for such regressive steps as the near dismantling of the estate tax.  Yes, there is too much money in politics.  The Supreme Court’s Citizens United decision, for example, was a wrong turn.  But these propositions do not add up to a chain of causality that can explain the root cause of our troubles let alone suggest how to fix them.

Consider four possible counterarguments to this oft-repeated theory.

  1. Being rich is not necessarily correlated with being right-wing or conservative. If anything, income and education these days are generally correlated with being liberal.  Blue states, for example, have higher incomes than red states.  Within states, blue counties have higher incomes than red counties.  This is not to deny that many of the ultra-rich, the upper 1/100 of 1%, have sold their souls to get their taxes cut.
  2. Donald Trump did not achieve his original rise from buffoon to president in 2016 via campaign contributions. The media gave him all the free publicity he needed.
  3. It is not even clear that campaign contributions have a major effect on electoral outcomes, especially in cases where the candidate is already well-known to the electorate. The deeply held belief to the contrary is not clearly supported by statistical evidence, as surprising as that sounds.  Admittedly, political donations could have an effect on politicians’ behavior even if they don’t get them elected.  For one thing, so long as politicians think that campaign spending affects electoral outcomes – which they do – they could cater to the wishes of their contributors.  For another thing, in the age of we must now take seriously the widespread suspicions that, even in the US, money goes into the politicians’  personal pockets and thus could have an effect on the behavior of our leaders without involving elections.  But the direct efficacy of campaign spending is less than widely believed.
  4. Liberal political activists, motivated by the inequality issue above all others, are in danger of getting swept up in the evident new momentum of the Far Left. Democratic Socialists are trending.  But support for such politicians in the primaries or as third-party candidates is a sure way to get Republicans elected.   The lesson should have been clear when Ralph Nader’s candidacy delivered the 2000 election to George W. Bush.   The median voter theorem still stands.

Even to the extent that political causality does run from inequality to conservative economic outcomes, the diagnosis doesn’t offer much of a road map for finding the way back.  What is the route to moderating income inequality and oligarchic tendencies? It is no good just saying that “we” should adopt more progressive economic policies.  Resentment of wealthy elites is already a big driver in electoral politics, but that does not necessarily lead to good electoral outcomes.  Donald Trump managed to hoodwink those voters who felt “left behind” into voting for him, by convincing them that he would fight for their interests.   Even though he did the opposite in this first term — cutting taxes for the rich and seeking to dismantle Obamacare — they elected him to a second.   They don’t vote their own interests.

Recent trends in technology have exacerbated long-standing patterns of perception bias. We used all to get our news from the same few TV networks.  Now, algorithms in social media steer the observer increasingly to the news sources that tell them what they want to hear, and the observer – while often aware of this fact in principle — fails to take into account that the information they are seeing with their own eyes has been heavily curated to reinforce their own prejudices.  Their systematic perception errors fail to allow for the possibility of sample selection bias.  This proposition is my inexpert attempt to group together the sources of systematic errors that psychologists and behavioral economists call the availability heuristic, anchoring, confirmation bias, and related phenomena.    The result, of course, is that we believe different facts.

Just because these psychological attributes are deeply imbedded in human brains (plausibly as an outcome of evolution), doesn’t mean they can’t be deliberately taken into account and corrected for.  Society can disapprove of social media’s engineering of algorithms that feed perception biases.  Students in school can be taught critical thinking. Don’t believe everything you hear. Don’t pass on gossip with higher confidence than it warrants.   Look for footnotes/links to reputable sources; and include such citations in what you write or tweet.  Don’t shy away from venues where people communicate with others outside their comfort zone.  Recognize that scientific statements coming from credentialed experts are likely to be more accurate than claims motivated by hatred for expertise and credentials.  Don’t assume that correlation necessarily implies causation.    Don’t jump from the proposition that something has particular undesirable effects to the proposition that all its effects are undesirable.

Prescriptions like these certainly sound like pie in the sky, far beyond what any realistic political strategy can hope to achieve.  But consider how much harder it is to carry out a political strategy of shifting resources away from wealthy elites.  Raising the political salience of inequality among the electorate won’t do it, because if the systematic perception errors are not attenuated, the strategy could continue to produce Trump votes as easily as support for progressive policies.  Trying to build support for platforms of policies like tax progressivity or universal health care won’t do it, if the public does not accurately perceive the effects of the policies.  And even where political campaigns in favor of specific progressive policies are successful at redistributing income, this will not necessarily generate a virtuous circle of restored political sanity and good policies.  The knock-on effects of reducing the flow of campaign contributions to conservatives do not have as much self-reinforcing power that some appear to believe.

It will remain an uphill battle.  But an effort to teach people to think critically would be self-reinforcing, as it would move the politicians and voters in the direction of support for science, expertise, and rationality. The effects would entail better policies and less income inequality as an outcome rather than as a purported starting point.
